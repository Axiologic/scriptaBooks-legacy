About Book

Public memory is uneven. Some atrocities are taught, filmed, and funded, while others remain underrepresented or denied. The book asks what follows for other victims when one crime becomes the lens for the rest, and when one memory becomes the default. Critics warn that Holocaust centrality can overshadow the Armenian Genocide, Rwanda, Cambodia, and Bosnia. Denial, and the political arguments that gather around remembrance, stay open.

Why does the murder of six million Jews occupy a unique place in global consciousness, and can that focus prevent future genocides or overshadow other victims? Did the law assembled after 1945 stop the next genocide? How do sovereignty and the cost of action bear on the duty to prevent? Can never again, a phrase that emerged from the Holocaust, cover every victim?

The path is comparison. It begins with the word Raphael Lemkin coined in 1944 and sets the Holocaust beside the Armenian Genocide, Cambodia, Rwanda, Bosnia, and the Holodomor. It examines state administration, antisemitic policy, the Nuremberg Laws of 1935, the Final Solution, European nationalism, eugenics, and colonial precedents. It also follows perpetrator documents, writing by Elie Wiesel, Primo Levi, and Anne Frank, film, museums, and schooling.

It is for readers who want Holocaust memory set beside other genocides, and who will stay with an unresolved question of remembrance rather than a finished pledge.

Whether never again can mean anyone is the tension these pages leave open.

What does it cost to remember one crime so completely, and who is still waiting?
