The Overlap That Confirms the Dacians

Confirmed Dacian history is the overlap and the limits of written, archaeological, and genetic records, not a continuous national biography.

What the records can confirm

A confirmed finding is not a plausible reading, and neither is a story the evidence does not yet support. That standard rules out a continuous national biography, a reconstructed Dacian religion, and a genetic definition of a modern people. The usable record is the overlap of unequal archives: ancient authors, excavation, and genome-wide ancient DNA where it exists. They can be joined only where each archive actually reaches.

History and archaeology can agree, independently of DNA, that a late kingdom built extraordinary mountain centers, fought Rome, and was replaced in 106 by a militarized province, and that the provincial name ended north of the Danube in the 270s and continued south of it. The Dacians are historically and archaeologically real. Their biological profile is not yet established, and no single test, text, or fortress can stand in for the others.

Names before a kingdom

Dacian is a classical name for communities across the Carpathians, Transylvania, the lower Danube, and neighboring plains, not proof of one state, one dialect, or one burial custom in every century. Strabo's claim that Getai and Daci were regional labels for speakers of the same language is the clearest ancient statement that the names belonged together. It is neither a census nor a map of political borders. Ptolemy and Pliny preserve names and are weak for exact frontiers, population figures, or the difference between kingdom, tributary district, and Roman province.

The language is a presence and a confirmed absence. Names, a few glosses, and plant names are too thin to settle a Paleo-Balkan relationship or to reconstruct speech, religious formulas, or a grammar. The stamp DECEBALUS PER SCORILO places a king's name in the material record, but reading PER as son of is a linguistic hypothesis, not a translation secured by a bilingual text. Modern Romanian is a Romance language whose non-Latin substrate, sometimes explained as Dacian or Thracian, cannot prove unchanged survival or that every Romanian speaker descends from the people Strabo called Dacians. A genetic similarity is not a language, a legal status, or a nationality.

From the first centuries BCE and CE the kingdom arose where settlement, metalworking, fortified hills, and contact with the steppe, the Balkans, and the Aegean were already in place. Communities north and south of the Danube used related but not identical pottery, burial rites, and settlement forms. Greek colonies traded and fought with Getic leaders, while inland evidence shows organization before Burebista without statements of ethnic identity. Scythian and later Sarmatian contacts do not make every Iron Age burial in Transylvania Scythian, or Dacian identity a simple mixture. Unpublished genome-wide conference data were not peer-reviewed when the book was written, and sampling of securely identified pre-Roman Dacians remains sparse.

Uneven witnesses

Greek and Roman writers are indispensable only at the strength each can bear. Herodotus's fifth-century account of the Getae, including Salmoxis, later spelled Zalmoxis or Zamolxis, is a Greek report rather than fieldwork. He neither fully believes nor wholly rejects the underground-chamber story, and he thinks Zalmoxis lived many years before Pythagoras. Where Strabo and Jordanes disagree in scale about Deceneus, Strabo is closer, though still a summary. Jordanes depends on Cassiodorus's lost Gothic history, identifies Goths with Getae, and is evidence for sixth-century Gothic historiography, not a Dacian priestly curriculum.

Ovid and Dio Chrysostom confirm that Roman readers knew the lower Danube as a dangerous frontier. A coastal vantage and a literary genre do not establish every inland custom. Cassius Dio supplies the backbone of Domitian's and Trajan's wars and preserves decisions archaeology cannot name by itself. He can be accepted where an event is specific and repeated by excavation or administration. Speeches, casualty figures, and insults without a second source remain Roman testimony. No Dacian narrative survives against which his numbers, motives, and moral judgments can be checked.

A kingdom Rome could destroy

Burebista is the first Dacian ruler who can be followed as a political actor. In the decades before Caesar's death he raised a large power with Deceneus, campaigned against the Boii, the Taurisci, and Greek coastal cities, and was overthrown at about the time Caesar was assassinated in 44 BCE. The kingdom then divided into four or five authorities. It changed the regional balance, but it did not survive him as a stable unified state. Strabo does not support a centralized empire, a standing bureaucracy, or a drawable capital plan, and projecting Sarmizegetusa Regia backward onto Burebista is an inference.

Settlements whose names include dava do not prove that every dava belonged to one king. The picture after Burebista is fragmentation, then a late first-century consolidation under rulers Roman sources treat as kings of the Dacians. By the 80s Decebalus was a frontier problem serious enough for legionary war, which ended only when Trajan destroyed the kingdom as a political unit. A stamped vessel ties that literary king to the capital area, while its second word remains debated. There is no confirmed native constitution, king list, or priestly code, and no basis for ethnic uniformity. Power could include subjects, allies, and displaced groups.

These best-dated wars remain Roman events. Conflict opened in 85 after Oppius Sabinus was defeated and killed. Cornelius Fuscus was also defeated. A later victory at Tapae, associated with Tettius Julianus, did not produce Trajan's conquest. Dio reports a peace in which Decebalus received money and technical help. The subsidy is transmitted history; Dio's moral verdict on Domitian belongs to a later dynasty. The war of 101 to 102 ended with a treaty limiting forces and foreign policy. The war of 105 to 106 followed Roman claims that he had rearmed, received deserters, and acted against neighbors. In 106 the capital was taken and Dacia became a province.

The report that Decebalus cut his own throat, and that his head was taken to Trajan, has no Dacian confirmation and no identified body. Ancient narrative stresses a broken treaty and a dangerous king. Modern explanations add Apuseni gold, fear of a strong Danube kingdom, Domitian's unfinished peace, and Trajan's politics, but a single mineral deposit is not confirmed as the sole cause. Casualty and prisoner numbers cited from Criton are unused because they are late and unverified. Roads, bridges, and supply decided the outcome. The Apulum preprint samples a provincial city, not the battlefield, and cannot date the wars.

Stone and a different province

The Orăștie group of six fortresses, including Sarmizegetusa Regia, was inscribed as a UNESCO property in 1999. Its murus Dacicus belongs to a Mediterranean and Hellenistic world as well as a local one. Terraces and andesite from quarries on the Mureș, and work in 2023 and 2024, show organized labor, but they do not date every terrace to a year of Decebalus's reign. A 2025 LiDAR study finds that natural processes and later activity changed terrace number and area. A 2023 gate study finds pre-conquest evidence too uneven to restore the original plan, and restoring an andesite temple is conservation, not a new discovery. Tools, pottery, hoards, and local copies of denarii show workshops and monetary contact, not a mint, a tax system, or a price list.

The province created in 106 and given up in the 270s was not the destroyed kingdom. Its capital was Ulpia Traiana Sarmizegetusa in the Hațeg depression, not the royal center in the Orăștie Mountains. It was heavily armed, with bases at Apulum and later Potaissa, posts including Porolissum, and roads tied to the Drobeta bridge. A 2025 study of Ungra adds a fort without rewriting the system. Apulum and the Alburnus Maior wax tablets confirm towns, mines, and a legally Roman working world whose names point to the Balkans and beyond, not every miner's origin. Eutropius says Trajan brought people from the whole Roman world because war had exhausted the men of the land, but he gives no number or list. Inscriptions overrepresent those who paid for stone and chose Latin.

Hadrian reorganized the territory, and by the later second century it was governed as the three Dacias, though the year of each adjustment is a specialist problem. Provincial religion is plural. Latin inscriptions honor Roman and provincial gods, and local divine names occur, while unchanged worship of Zalmoxis beside the Roman pantheon is rarely demonstrated. A 2026 genetic preprint's mention of women's religious activity and local cults is historical context, not a genome or a newly excavated temple.

What remains unclosed

A genome can show relative shared ancestry, not language, a Roman legal label, or a modern nationality. No published genome names a person as Dacian, and no published genome-wide study samples a cemetery securely identifiable as a cross-section of the pre-Roman kingdom. Unpublished conference presentations are not confirmed facts. Olalde's 2023 study of 136 individuals, mainly from Serbia and Croatia, is not a Dacian sample. Its models assign later Eastern European-related ancestry roughly 30 to 60 percent of Balkan groups and about 50 to 60 percent in models of present-day Serbs, Croats, Bulgarians, and Romanians, beside Iron Age Balkan-related ancestry and, in some models, an Anatolian-related share. Those are model results, not a headcount or a percentage of modern identity. They rule out reading 106 as a biological ending, show little specifically Italic ancestry in the Imperial period, and find a larger Slavic-related shift especially after about 700.

The April 2026 Apulum preprint was not peer-reviewed when the book was written. Of 34 sequenced individuals, 14 with at least 10,000 overlapping SNPs remained for the main analysis. No close related pair was found in that set, and Y-chromosome haplogroups could be called for only three men. Both readings of the female signal must be kept: recent arrivals from eastern and steppe-connected groups, or local women whose ancestry already carried those connections before 106. The data do not decide, and burial location can affect the pattern. The result fits immigration and a mixed Roman city, including male burials with Mediterranean and North African-related ancestry. It does not measure the pre-Roman kingdom or the period after withdrawal, and it must not be averaged with Olalde's Balkan models and renamed the Dacian genome.

Roman administration north of the Danube is conventionally said to end under Aurelian in 271, though the change may have lasted more than one season. Eutropius says the army and provincials left a devastated Dacia and settled south of the river in a new province of the same name. Later geography distinguishes Dacia Ripensis and Dacia Mediterranea. The name moved with the administration. It does not prove that every person north of the river left or stayed. The Costoboci reached Greece in 170, and the Carpi, a major name in third-century wars, were later settled in part inside the empire. Free Dacians is modern shorthand, not a second kingdom with a capital and a king list. Continuity has to be shown site by site, not deduced from a classical name.

Daco-Roman continuity is a real question, but neither that claim nor its denial is a national verdict. A Romance language in Romania, roughly a century and a half of Roman rule north of the Danube, and medieval marks across the Balkans can coexist with immigration and language shift. No confirmed statement identifies a living person as a Dacian. Zalmoxis as certainly a god, a man, or a shaman discards Herodotus's caution. Gebeleizis is only a name used by some Getae, and the spear ritual is not independently dated. Terraces, structures called sanctuaries, and the andesite disk are stones, not rites. No text says what happened on the tenth or eleventh terrace. Trajan's Column, dedicated in 113, and the bridge associated with Apollodorus of Damascus show Roman works, crossings, sieges, a curved blade, and the draco. The Column is not a map, the disk is not a calendar, and the draco is not a national wolf cult.

Confirmed Dacian history is that overlap and those limits, not a continuous national biography. Authors, excavation, and sparse ancient DNA meet only where each archive actually reaches. The Dacians are historically and archaeologically real. Their biological profile is not yet established, and a genetic similarity is not a language, a legal status, or a nationality.

Withdrawal moved a name without counting who stayed, and without measuring how much of a nation comes from Decebalus. A Roman city and Balkan models cannot yet name or continue the Dacians. Cult, calendar, wolf standard, and unbroken descent remain stories the evidence does not support. Until each record is used only at its own strength, similarity will keep being mistaken for a people.
