A famous ancient name, held to the limits of three unequal records

A careful look at what ancient writers, excavations, and genetic studies can be asked, and at the limits those records do not cross.

A name that is easy to overclaim

Greek and Latin writers left a name for communities and a kingdom Rome later conquered. The request behind this book was narrow enough that a continuous national biography would change the task.

Two emperors fought the wars treated here as the best dated events, yet they remain Roman events. A bridge or an imperial column still invites a match that may not hold. Ancient DNA, the newest record, is the easiest to overread.

Questions the records still leave open

What can the names, the language, and the early written descriptions actually carry, and can an ancient geographer's sentence serve as a map? Can earlier histories of settlement and fortified hills be compressed into a single people waiting to be named? Was the kingdom ethnically uniform, or only named that way by a foreign state?

Was the Roman capital north of the Danube the same place as the old royal center? What can a genome not show about a language or a modern nation? Did the name move when administration ended?

Three archives, kept unequal

The account separates a confirmed finding from a plausible reading and from a story the evidence does not yet support. Ancient authors, excavation, and genome-wide ancient DNA are set side by side.

Excavation reports and a LiDAR study are asked whether they close the larger arguments, while ancient accounts, archaeology, and genetics stay in separate groups so their weight remains visible.

Who this careful history is for

It is for general readers, students, teachers, and researchers who want genetic claims and continuity debates held to the same standard as texts and excavations, and who want ancient history argued from its own evidence. That standard is the invitation, and also the limit of the promise.
