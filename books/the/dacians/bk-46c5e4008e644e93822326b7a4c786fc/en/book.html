The Dacians

This book is about the people whom Greek and Latin writers called Dacians and Getae, the kingdom that Rome conquered in 106 CE, and the limits of what can now be confirmed about them. It is written for general readers, students, teachers, and researchers who want the same standard: a clear account that separates a confirmed finding from a plausible reading and from a story that the evidence does not yet support.

The request behind the book was narrow. It asked for Dacian history based on recent archaeological discoveries, correlated with DNA tests and written historical sources, and restricted to confirmed facts. That restriction changes the shape of the book. A continuous national biography, a reconstructed Dacian religion, or a genetic definition of a modern people would go beyond the sources. The confirmed record is instead a set of overlapping but unequal archives. Ancient authors describe some events and misunderstand others. Excavation and survey show how particular places were built, burned, rebuilt, and abandoned. Genome-wide ancient DNA, where it exists, describes ancestry in a sampled cemetery, not the identity of everyone who lived north of the Danube.

Three cautions apply throughout. First, “Dacian” is a classical name used for communities spread across the Carpathians, Transylvania, the lower Danube, and neighboring plains. It is not proof that those communities shared one state, one dialect, or one burial custom in every century. Second, the Roman province of Dacia, created in 106 and given up in the 270s, was not the same thing as the pre-Roman kingdom, and the Roman colony Ulpia Traiana Sarmizegetusa was not the Dacian capital in the Orăștie Mountains. Third, a genetic similarity is not a language, a legal status, or a nationality. Those categories have to be argued from their own evidence.

The chapters follow time, but each one is also a test of a kind of evidence. The opening chapters ask what the names, the language, and the early written descriptions can actually carry. The middle chapters follow the kingdom, the mountain fortresses, and the Roman wars with the newest published fieldwork kept in view. The later chapters put the Roman province, the first genome-wide study of a Roman Dacian cemetery, and the period after the Roman withdrawal side by side. The closing chapter states, as plainly as possible, where the three records agree, where they cannot yet be joined, and which familiar claims remain unconfirmed.

Names, lands, and a poorly preserved language

Greek writers usually said Getai. Latin writers usually said Daci. Strabo, a geographer working in the Augustan age, treated the two names as regional labels for people who spoke the same language: Dacians toward the German and Danube-source side, Getae toward the Black Sea. That sentence is the clearest ancient statement that the names belonged together. It is not a census, and it is not a map of political borders. Strabo was summarizing a large and uneven ethnographic tradition, not reporting a survey he had made in the Carpathians.

The land those names pointed to is easier to bound in broad terms than in administrative ones. The Carpathian arc, the Transylvanian plateau, the Mureș and Olt corridors, the Danube gorges, and the plains running toward the Black Sea and the Tisza all appear in the classical geography of the Getae and Dacians. Ptolemy’s Geography later lists towns in a Roman-era Dacia, and Pliny’s Natural History registers Dacians among the peoples of the Danube. Those lists are valuable because they preserve names. They are weak where a modern reader wants exact frontiers, population figures, or a distinction between a kingdom, a tributary district, and a Roman province. Ancient geographical writing often stacked names from different dates into one description.

The language is a confirmed absence as much as a confirmed presence. No Dacian book, law code, or long inscription survives. What remains are personal names, place names, a small number of glosses, and plant names preserved by Greek medical writers. Scholars classify this material as Paleo-Balkan and discuss its relationship to Thracian. The evidence is too thin to decide that question with the confidence sometimes claimed in reference works. It is also too thin to reconstruct daily speech, religious formulas, or a grammar. A stamped vessel from the Sarmizegetusa area reading DECEBALUS PER SCORILO is a real and important object, because it places a king’s name in the material record. The common reading of PER as “son of” is a linguistic hypothesis, not a translation secured by a bilingual text. The book therefore reports the stamp and does not treat the patronymic reading as settled.

Modern Romanian is a Romance language. Its non-Latin substrate has been explained in more than one way, including a Dacian or Thracian contribution. Substrate words and place names can show that older languages left traces. They cannot, by themselves, prove that a particular ancient kingdom’s population survived unchanged, or that every speaker of Romanian descends from the people Strabo called Dacians. That is a historical argument, and the chapters on the Roman province and the period after 271 show why it cannot be settled from language alone.

The practical consequence is modest and important. In this book, “Dacian” means the communities and kingdom identified by classical sources and by the archaeology of late Iron Age and early Roman sites in this region. “Getae” is retained when an ancient author uses that name. Neither word is used as a genetic label.

Before the kingdom

The Dacian kingdom known from the first centuries BCE and CE did not appear in an empty landscape. The Carpathian and lower Danube regions already had long histories of settlement, metalworking, fortified hills, and contact with the steppe, the Balkans, and the Aegean. Those earlier histories matter because later writers, and some modern summaries, compress them into a single Dacian people waiting to be named.

Archaeology does not support that compression. Late Bronze Age and Iron Age communities north and south of the Danube used related but not identical pottery, burial rites, and settlement forms. Greek colonies on the western Black Sea coast, including Histria, Tomis, and Callatis, traded and fought with neighboring Getic leaders. Their inscriptions and the Greek historians who noticed the coast give a partial view of those relationships. Inland, the evidence is more archaeological than literary. Hillforts, tumuli, and metal deposits show organized communities and long-distance exchange well before Burebista. They do not come with statements of ethnic identity.

Steppe contacts are part of this earlier record, but they need careful wording. Classical authors placed Scythians and, later, Sarmatians on the plains east and south of the Carpathians. Archaeology finds mobile-pastoralist material in some of the same regions. Neither fact authorizes a claim that every Iron Age burial in Transylvania was Scythian, or that Dacian identity was a simple mixture of one local and one steppe population. Conference abstracts have announced unpublished genome-wide data from Iron Age Transylvania, including burials associated with “Scythian” material. Those data were not a peer-reviewed, publicly analyzed dataset at the time this book was written. They are therefore not used here as confirmed results. The confirmed statement is narrower: published genome-wide sampling of people who can be securely identified as pre-Roman Dacians remains sparse, and the kingdom’s population history cannot yet be read from DNA in the way the Roman wars can be read from Cassius Dio.

What can be confirmed about the centuries before the kingdom is a pattern of regional power rather than a biography. Communities in the mountains and along the rivers could gather labor for fortification and craft production. They took part in wider networks that carried Greek goods inland and Carpathian resources outward. They were known to Greek observers mainly when they affected the coast or when a striking religious story traveled. The next chapter stays with those observers, because their stories are both indispensable and easy to overread.

What Greeks and Romans thought they knew

The oldest extended description is Herodotus, writing in the fifth century BCE. He calls the Getae the bravest and most just of the Thracians. He says they believed they did not really die, but went to a being he calls Salmoxis, a name later writers spell Zalmoxis or Zamolxis. He describes a ritual in which a man chosen as a messenger was thrown onto upturned spears, and another in which the Getae shot arrows at thunder and lightning while threatening the god. He also reports a Greek story from the Hellespont and the Black Sea: that Zalmoxis had been a slave of Pythagoras, had learned Greek ideas of immortality, and had deceived the Getae by hiding in an underground chamber and returning. Herodotus does not simply accept that story. He says he neither fully believes nor wholly rejects what is said about the chamber, and he thinks Zalmoxis lived many years before Pythagoras.

That passage is confirmed as a fifth-century Greek account. It is not confirmed as a fieldwork report. Herodotus did not claim to have watched the ritual. The archaeological record has not produced an independent, dated demonstration that the spear ritual occurred as he describes it. The value of the passage is nevertheless high. It shows that Getic religion was already famous, that Greeks explained it by attaching it to Pythagoras, and that a careful ancient author could separate a report from his own confidence in it. Later claims that Zalmoxis was certainly a god, certainly a man, or certainly a shaman borrow Herodotus and then discard his caution. This book keeps the caution. It also keeps the other name Herodotus records, Gebeleizis, as a name used by some Getae, without inventing a second theology to go with it.

Roman and Greek writers of the late Republic and early Empire add politics to religion. Strabo is the principal source for Burebista and for the adviser he calls Dekaineos, usually rendered Deceneus. Strabo presents Deceneus as the man who encouraged discipline, sobriety, and obedience, and who gave the king a religious authority over his people. Jordanes, writing in the sixth century, expands this figure into a culture hero who taught the Dacians astronomy, ethics, and almost every civilized art. Jordanes is not an independent witness. His Getica depends on a lost Gothic history by Cassiodorus and deliberately identifies Goths with Getae. His expansion of Deceneus is evidence for sixth-century Gothic historiography, not for the curriculum of a Dacian priesthood. Where Strabo and Jordanes disagree in scale, Strabo is the closer source, and even Strabo is a summary.

Ovid, exiled to Tomis in the early first century CE, is a different kind of witness. His Tristia and Letters from the Black Sea describe cold, raids, and Getic neighbors in verse written to win a recall to Rome. The poems confirm that a Roman literary audience was expected to recognize the lower Danube as a dangerous frontier. They do not confirm the customs of every community inland, and they are shaped by the genre of exile complaint. Dio Chrysostom, also associated with Tomis, likewise shows a Greek intellectual looking at Getic neighbors from the coast. Neither writer replaces excavation in the mountains.

The most important political narrative is Cassius Dio, a senator writing in the early third century with access to Roman historical tradition. His account of Domitian’s and Trajan’s Dacian wars is the backbone of the chronology in later chapters. It is a Roman account. It explains wars through treaties broken, emperors insulted, and enemies defeated. It preserves decisions and outcomes that archaeology cannot name by itself. It also uses numbers, motives, and moral judgments that cannot be checked against a Dacian narrative, because no such narrative survives. The method used here is to accept Dio where the event is specific and repeated by the archaeological or administrative record, and to label as Roman testimony the speeches, casualty figures, and insults that have no second source.

From Burebista to Decebalus

The first Dacian ruler who can be followed as a political actor is Burebista. Strabo says that in the decades before Caesar’s death he raised a large power, with Deceneus as his religious and disciplinary partner, and that he campaigned against Celtic groups, including the Boii and Taurisci, and against Greek cities on the coast. Strabo also says that Caesar was preparing a campaign against him, and that Burebista was overthrown by his own people at about the time Caesar was assassinated in 44 BCE. The kingdom then divided. In Augustus’s time, Strabo reports four or five separate authorities where one king had ruled.

Those statements support a limited conclusion. A powerful Getic or Dacian ruler in the mid-first century BCE changed the balance among Celts, Greeks, and Romans in the lower Danube. His power did not survive him as a stable unified state. They do not support a map of a centralized empire with known provinces, a standing bureaucracy, or a capital whose plan we can draw for his reign. Sarmizegetusa Regia, in the Orăștie Mountains, is the confirmed capital of the later kingdom confronted by Trajan. Projecting that capital backward onto Burebista as a fully documented royal seat is an inference, not a statement in Strabo.

The century between Burebista’s fall and Decebalus is only partly lit. Roman writers notice Dacian and Getic groups when they cross the Danube or when Rome intervenes. Archaeology shows continued occupation and construction in the mountain zone and at many valley settlements whose names survive in Ptolemy or in later inscriptions, often with the element dava. The dava names are evidence that a settlement vocabulary existed. They are not, by themselves, evidence that every dava belonged to one king. The honest chronological picture is fragmentation after Burebista, local and regional power, and a new consolidation by the late first century CE under rulers whom Roman sources treat as kings of the Dacians.

Decebalus is the king of that later consolidation. Dio presents him as a formidable opponent of Domitian and Trajan, able to win battles, negotiate subsidies, and rebuild strength after a peace. The stamped vessel bearing his name ties the literary king to the material world of the capital area, even while the second word of the stamp remains debated. There is no confirmed Dacian account of his birth, his route to power, or his administration. What is confirmed is that by the 80s CE Rome treated a Dacian king as a frontier problem serious enough for legionary war, and that the war ended only when Trajan destroyed the kingdom as a political unit.

Two negative findings belong in this chapter because they are often skipped. There is no confirmed native written constitution, king list, or priestly code. There is also no confirmed basis for treating the Dacian kingdom as ethnically uniform. The military power that Rome encountered could include subjects, allies, and displaced groups. Classical language of “the Dacians” is the language of a foreign state naming an enemy. It should be quoted as such.

Stone, terraces, and sanctuaries

The strongest material evidence for the late kingdom stands in the Orăștie Mountains of southwestern Transylvania. Six fortresses, including Sarmizegetusa Regia, Costești-Cetățuie, Costești-Blidaru, Luncani-Piatra Roșie, Bănița, and Căpâlna, form the UNESCO World Heritage property inscribed in 1999 as the Dacian Fortresses of the Orăștie Mountains. Their walls use a technique often called murus Dacicus: two faces of dressed stone, a rubble core, and timber ties binding the faces. The technique belongs to a Mediterranean and Hellenistic building world as well as to a local one. That combination is itself a confirmed historical fact. The kingdom was not isolated from the architectural knowledge of its neighbors.

Sarmizegetusa Regia was both a fortress and a sacred landscape of artificial terraces. On those terraces stood rectangular and circular structures that archaeologists call temples or sanctuaries, together with an andesite disk whose radiating pieces have attracted calendar and solar interpretations. The stones are confirmed. The rituals are not. No Dacian text tells a modern visitor what happened on the tenth or eleventh terrace. Interpretations that assign each monument a single astronomical function, or that identify every round building with Zalmoxis, go beyond the evidence. The andesite itself is informative in a different way. It was brought from quarries on the Mureș, a long and difficult haul into the mountains. Whatever the cult was, the kingdom could move heavy stone, organize quarries, and rebuild sacred structures more than once.

Recent fieldwork has added details without closing the larger arguments. In 2023 and 2024, teams from the National Museum of Transylvanian History and partner institutions continued excavation on the ninth terrace at Grădiștea de Munte–Sarmizegetusa Regia. The museum’s reports describe an alignment-type temple, limestone bases, delimiting structures, and an earlier phase that ended in a fire, with debris spread over a large area. At Piatra Roșie in 2024, work on the fortification plateau documented a temple and a building first identified in the 1949 excavations led by Constantin Daicoviciu. At Costești-Cetățuie, the same 2024 season exposed the limestone foundations of a cult building that had not been known before, on a southeastern terrace. These are confirmed additions to the plan. They do not yet yield a complete chronology of every terrace, and they do not identify which structure, if any, was standing in a particular year of Decebalus’s reign.

Other new work is about how much of the ancient landscape can be seen at all. A 2025 study in the Journal of Ancient History and Archaeology uses LiDAR to reconsider the number and condition of anthropogenic terraces from Grădiștea de Munte toward Costești. Its published abstract stresses that both natural processes and later human activity have changed the number and the surface area of terraces since antiquity. A precise modern count is therefore not a simple window onto the Dacian census. A 2023 architectural and archaeological study of the eastern gate area, by Virgil Apostol and Răzvan Mateescu, makes a related point about the fortress itself: evidence for a fortified site before the Roman conquest exists, but it is so uneven that recomposing the original plan remains difficult. Restoration work announced in 2025 for the andesite temple on the tenth terrace, including reassembly of column elements, is a conservation project. It should not be confused with the discovery of a new temple.

Outside the sacred terraces, the kingdom’s economy is visible in iron tools, pottery, and precious-metal work. Hoards of tools show specialized blacksmithing. Imported and local coinage, including local copies of Roman denarii, shows that the kingdom sat inside Roman monetary circuits. A full reconstruction of a Dacian mint, tax system, or price list is not confirmed. The confirmed economic picture is of a mountain power with workshops, stored wealth, and the ability to draw on lowland resources, living close enough to the Roman world to copy its coins and, eventually, to fight its legions.

Two emperors, two wars

The wars that ended the kingdom are the best dated events in this book, and they are still Roman events. Under Domitian, conflict with the Dacians opened in 85 CE after the governor Oppius Sabinus was defeated and killed. Cornelius Fuscus, the praetorian prefect, led a further expedition and was also defeated. A later Roman victory at Tapae, associated with Tettius Julianus, did not produce the conquest later achieved by Trajan. Dio reports a peace in which Decebalus received money and technical help, and he presents Domitian’s celebration of the war as dishonest. The subsidy and the bitter senatorial memory of it are part of the transmitted history. The moral verdict on Domitian is Dio’s, written under a later dynasty that had reasons to diminish Domitian.

Trajan’s first Dacian war, in 101–102, ended with a treaty that limited Decebalus’s forces and foreign policy and required him to accept Roman terms. The second war, in 105–106, followed Roman claims that he had broken those terms by rearming, receiving deserters, and acting against neighbors. In 106 the capital was taken, the kingdom ceased to exist as an independent state, and Dacia became a Roman province. Dio says that Decebalus cut his own throat when capture was near, and that his head was taken to Trajan. That is the Roman account of the king’s death. There is no Dacian confirmation and no identified body. The event can be reported only as Dio reports it.

The Danube bridge built for these campaigns, associated with the architect Apollodorus of Damascus, is independently visible as a major Roman work at Drobeta. Trajan’s Column in Rome, dedicated in 113, preserves the longest visual narrative of the wars: river crossings, sieges, embassies, a curved Dacian blade, and the dragon or wolf-headed draco standard. The Column is a confirmed monument and an imperial monument. It selects scenes that justify conquest. It is not a photograph, and matching a particular spiral to a particular hill in the Orăștie Mountains has often been tempting and often unprovable. Archaeological destruction layers in the Dacian fortresses are consistent with violent conquest and Roman demolition. They rarely carry a label that says which legion set the fire.

Why Rome fought is narrower than legend. Gold in the Apuseni Mountains, fear of a strong kingdom on the Danube, the unfinished business of Domitian’s peace, and Trajan’s imperial politics all appear in modern explanations. Ancient narrative emphasizes a broken treaty and a dangerous king. The gold of Alburnus Maior, today’s Roșia Montană, certainly mattered to the later province, as the mining settlement and its famous wax tablets show. It is not confirmed that a single mineral deposit was the sole cause of the war. Large casualty and prisoner numbers transmitted by later authors who cited Trajan’s doctor Criton are not used here. They are late, enormous, and not independently verified.

The wars confirm something else that is easy to miss. Rome did not encounter a vague tribe. It encountered fortified centers, a king who could negotiate and rearm, and a landscape in which roads, bridges, and supply decided the outcome. The Dacian side of that military organization is known mainly from its works and from the enemy’s reliefs. That asymmetry is a fact about the sources, not a reason to fill the gap with a reconstructed general staff.

A province north of the Danube

After 106, Roman Dacia was a province on the far side of the river, connected to the rest of the empire by the Drobeta bridge and by roads running into the mountains and across the plateau. Its Roman capital in the legal sense was not the destroyed royal center. Ulpia Traiana Sarmizegetusa, in the Hațeg depression, was a colony founded for the new province. Sarmizegetusa Regia remained the old mountain site. Keeping the two names apart is a basic condition of accuracy.

The province was heavily armed. Legio XIII Gemina was based at Apulum, modern Alba Iulia. Legio V Macedonica later lay at Potaissa. Forts and roads tied the legionary bases to auxiliary posts, including the northern frontier zone around Porolissum. A 2025 multidisciplinary study of the Roman fort at Ungra, in southeastern Transylvania, using satellite imagery, drone survey, and geophysics, is a reminder that the military map is still being filled in. One newly analyzed fort does not rewrite the provincial system. It shows that confirmed knowledge of that system still depends on survey as well as on texts.

Civilian Dacia is known from towns, inscriptions, mines, and a few extraordinary documents. Apulum grew from a legionary fortress and its civilian settlement into one of the province’s main cities. Alburnus Maior produced gold and, in the wax tablets discovered from the eighteenth century onward, contracts and names from a mining community. The dated tablets cluster in the second century, many of them under Hadrian and Antoninus Pius. They confirm a literate, legally Roman working world in the Apuseni Mountains, with people whose names point to the Balkans and beyond. They do not confirm the origin of every miner, and they say nothing directly about families who never made a contract.

Eutropius, a fourth-century abbreviator, writes that Trajan brought large numbers of people from the whole Roman world to cultivate the fields and cities of Dacia, because the long war with Decebalus had exhausted the men of the land. This sentence has carried great weight in arguments about whether the provincial population was mostly new. It is a real source and a short one. It does not give a number, a list of cities of origin, or a description of the people who remained. Inscriptions support the underlying picture of immigration: soldiers, veterans, officials, and civilians with homes elsewhere erected monuments in Dacia, as they did in other frontier provinces. Inscriptions overrepresent people who could pay for stone and who chose Latin. They are poor evidence for the rural majority.

Administration changed more than once. Hadrian reorganized the conquered territory into more than one provincial unit, and by the later second century the region was governed as the three Dacias, with the consular governor at Apulum. The exact year of each adjustment is a specialist problem. The confirmed pattern is enough for this book: Dacia was not left as a single untouched wartime command, and its government sat in a militarized landscape.

Religion in the province is also plural in the evidence that survives. Latin inscriptions honor Roman and provincial gods. Local divine names occur. The idea that worship of Zalmoxis simply continued, unchanged, beside the Roman pantheon is often repeated and rarely demonstrated from a clear inscription or sanctuary. A 2026 genetic preprint discussed in the next chapter cites women’s religious activity and local cults as historical context, but that context is not the same thing as a genome, and it is not a new excavation of a Zalmoxis temple. The confirmed religious statement is that provincial Dacia shared the mixed divine world of other Roman frontiers, while the pre-Roman cults of the Orăștie terraces remain largely unnamed.

What the genomes can confirm

Ancient DNA is the newest of the three records, and it is the easiest to overread. A genome can show that a buried person shared more ancestry with one set of ancient populations than with another. It cannot show what language that person spoke, whether a Roman official called that person Dacian, or whether a modern nation is the same people. Those limits are not a disappointment added at the end. They are the conditions under which the confirmed genetic facts have to be stated.

The first limitation is sampling. As of this writing, there is no published genome-wide study of a cemetery that can be securely identified as a cross-section of the pre-Roman Dacian kingdom. Unpublished conference presentations have described Iron Age and Roman-period sampling in Transylvania. Until the data, dates, archaeological contexts, and quality filters are published and can be checked, they are not confirmed facts for this book. The absence is itself important. Anyone who offers a genetic portrait of “the Dacians” before the Roman conquest is going beyond the published genomic record.

The published Balkan study that readers are most likely to meet does not fill that gap. In 2023, Iñigo Olalde and colleagues reported genome-wide data from 136 individuals of the first millennium CE, excavated mainly in Serbia and Croatia, including the Roman frontier city of Viminacium. The paper is about the Balkans from the Roman frontier to the Slavic migrations. It is not a Dacian sample. Its Imperial-period finding is still relevant as a comparison. Despite the military culture of the frontier, the authors found little ancestry specifically from people of Italic descent, and they traced a substantial influx of Anatolian-related ancestry during the Imperial period. After Roman rule, especially after about 700 CE, they detected people genetically similar to present-day Eastern European Slavic-speaking populations. In their models, that later ancestry contributed roughly 30 to 60 percent of the ancestry of Balkan groups, and present-day Serbs, Croats, Bulgarians, and Romanians could be modeled with a similar mixture, including about 50 to 60 percent Eastern European-related ancestry alongside Iron Age Balkan-related ancestry and, in some models, an Anatolian-related share.

Those figures are model results, not a headcount of ancient Dacians and not a percentage of modern identity. The same paper says that the evidence rules out a complete replacement of earlier Balkan populations. A present-day Romanian model in a Balkan study cannot be quoted as a test of Dacian survival. It can be quoted for a smaller, solid conclusion: the people who live in this part of Europe today carry layers of ancestry from more than one period, including a major early-medieval Eastern European-related contribution shared with neighboring countries. Any account that stops at 106 CE, or that treats 106 as a biological ending, is incomplete. Any account that treats a medieval migration as the erasure of everyone already there is also incompatible with the published model.

The study that does sample Roman Dacia is a preprint, and it must be labeled as one. In April 2026, Flavio De Angelis, Ileana Buzic, and colleagues posted genome-wide results from the Roman necropolis at Apulum–Dealul Furcilor, Alba Iulia. They sequenced 34 individuals. After requiring at least 10,000 overlapping SNPs with their reference panel, 14 individuals remained for the main population-genetic analysis. No close related pair was found among those with enough overlapping data. The preprint had not been peer-reviewed when this book was written. Its authors also warn that one cemetery, in a city likely to contain many newcomers, may not represent Roman Dacia, and that a sex-biased pattern can be affected by who was buried where.

Within those limits, the reported pattern is specific. The 14 individuals do not form a tight cluster. They sit across a genetic space connecting Eastern Europe, the Mediterranean, and western Asia. In the authors’ clustering, women in the analyzed set show a larger share of a component also prominent in steppe and Caucasus-related ancient groups, while men show larger shares of components prominent in Mediterranean and Punic-related groups. Outgroup statistics place the women closest, in that analysis, to Late Bronze Age and Iron Age groups including Armenia_Ura and Moldova_LBA, and place the men closest to groups such as Spain_Pun, with other high values for Italy_Pun, Serbia_Roman, and Moldova_IA. Y-chromosome haplogroups could be called for only three men: J-BY37605, R-Y52, and E-CTS1273. Mitochondrial lineages in the women included branches of U and H; the men mostly carried H lineages. The authors offer two historical readings of the female signal, and both should be kept: either women at this cemetery included recent arrivals from eastern and steppe-connected groups, or they included local women whose ancestry already had those older connections before 106. The data as published do not decide between those readings. They also do not identify any individual as a Dacian in the legal or linguistic sense.

What can be correlated, cautiously, is this. Written sources and inscriptions already said that Roman Dacia received soldiers, veterans, miners, and other immigrants, and Eutropius said the resettlement was large. The Apulum preprint is consistent with a genetically mixed Roman city, and with male burials that include Mediterranean and North African-related ancestry of the kind seen in other Roman-period networks. It does not measure the pre-Roman kingdom. It does not prove that every woman in the cemetery was a local Dacian, which the authors themselves do not claim as a closed finding. It does not show what happened after the Roman withdrawal, because the cemetery belongs to the provincial period. Used at its real strength, it confirms diversity at one major Roman Dacian city and forbids any simple equation of “Roman Dacia” with one genetic type.

After the province

Roman administration north of the Danube ended under Aurelian. The conventional date is 271, and the change may have taken more than a single season. Eutropius says that Aurelian withdrew the army and the provincials from a Dacia that had been devastated, and settled them south of the Danube in a new province that also carried the name Dacia. Later geography distinguishes Dacia Ripensis and Dacia Mediterranea on the southern bank. The name therefore moved with Roman administration. That administrative fact is confirmed. The idea that every inhabitant north of the river either left or stayed is not. Eutropius is describing a provincial relocation as understood in the fourth century, not taking a census of farmers in the eastern Carpathians.

People whom Roman sources continued to call Dacian lived outside the provincial borders even while the province existed. The Costoboci raided into the Balkans during the Marcomannic wars and reached Greece in 170. The Carpi became a major name in third-century wars and were later settled, in part, inside the empire. Roman writers could use “Dacian” for these groups. Archaeology and names do not let us treat every one of them as a province of Decebalus’s old kingdom that had simply remained free. “Free Dacians” is a useful modern shorthand for peoples north and east of Roman Dacia whom Romans associated with the Dacian name. It is not a confirmed second kingdom with a capital, a king list, and a continuous administration.

The following centuries belong to a wider migration period. Goths appear in the written record of the lower Danube. Olalde and colleagues, sampling the Balkans rather than the old Dacian capital, found Central and Northern European-related and steppe-related migrants in late antiquity, and then a much larger Eastern European-related shift after about 700. Roman Dacia itself is not the sampling area of that paper. The responsible comparison is regional: the Carpathian-Danubian world did not stop changing in 271, and genetic continuity, where it is claimed, has to be demonstrated site by site rather than deduced from a classical name.

Later interpretations have asked this early evidence to do modern work. Romanian historical writing has long debated a theory of Daco-Roman continuity, in which Latin-speaking provincial populations remained north of the Danube and became the core of the Romanians. Competing political arguments have denied that continuity in order to deny a Romanian priority in Transylvania. Both arguments use place names, Latin inscriptions, archaeology, and, more recently, DNA. The confirmed scholarly position is that the question is real and the shortcut is not. A Romance language is spoken in Romania. Roman administration north of the Danube lasted about a century and a half. Medieval migrations left a genetic and historical mark across the Balkans. None of these statements, alone or in a slogan, identifies a living person as a Dacian or measures the proportion of a nation that “comes from” Decebalus. This book stops where the evidence stops. Continuity of some communities, immigration, and language shift can all be true in the same landscape. They have to be proven for particular places and periods, not won as a single national verdict.

Where the records fit, and where they do not

The written sources, the excavations, and the genetic studies answer different questions. Putting them in one table makes the confirmed overlap visible and makes the gaps harder to ignore.

What each record can currently confirm
Question Written sources Archaeology Ancient DNA
Were there people called Getae or Dacians? Yes. Herodotus, Strabo, Dio, and geographical writers use the names. Late Iron Age sites and the Orăștie fortresses match the kingdom those writers fought and described. No published genome identifies a person as Dacian by name.
Was there a kingdom, and when did Rome destroy it? Yes. Burebista is in Strabo; Decebalus and the wars of 85–89, 101–102, and 105–106 are in Dio. The province begins in 106. Fortresses, terraces, destruction, and Roman military works agree with a violent conquest and a provincial system. Not a dating tool for the wars. The Apulum preprint samples the provincial city, not the battlefield.
Did Rome settle outsiders in the province? Eutropius says the transfer was large. Inscriptions name soldiers and civilians from elsewhere. Colonies, forts, mines, and the Alburnus Maior tablets show a Roman institutional landscape. The 2026 Apulum preprint, not yet peer-reviewed, finds a mixed cemetery and a sex-biased ancestry pattern in 14 analyzed people. It may not represent the province.
Can pre-Roman Dacian ancestry be described? No. Ancient authors do not describe biological ancestry. Material culture shows regional connections, not genomes. Not from a published, securely attributed sample set. Unpublished announcements are not used here.
Do present-day Romanians genetically equal ancient Dacians? No ancient source could ask this. No. No. Balkan models include later Eastern European-related ancestry and also earlier layers. They are not a Dacian percentage.

Read across the rows, the correlation the subject demands is possible only in part. History and archaeology agree that a Dacian kingdom in the late first century BCE and the first century CE built extraordinary mountain centers, fought Rome, and was replaced in 106 by a militarized province with new cities and a large immigrant presence. They agree that the provincial name ended north of the Danube in the 270s and continued south of it. They do not agree because DNA forced them to. They agree because texts and places have been studied together for a long time.

DNA adds a new kind of agreement, and only inside a Roman city. The Apulum preprint fits the written picture of a frontier capital filled with people from more than one region, and it adds a possible difference between the ancestry of the women and the men who could be analyzed. It does not identify the pre-Roman population of the Orăștie sanctuaries. It should not be averaged with Olalde’s Balkan models and then renamed “the Dacian genome.” Those studies sample different centuries and, largely, different countries. The latest confirmed genetic statement about this subject is therefore modest. Roman Dacia, at one well-known cemetery, looks genetically mixed. The Dacians of the kingdom are not yet a published genomic population. Present-day populations in the wider region are layered, with a major early-medieval component that no account of 106 can absorb by silence.

Several well-known claims fail that standard and are listed so they are not smuggled back in. It is not confirmed that Zalmoxis was a single historical reformer whose cult can be reconstructed from Herodotus. It is not confirmed that the andesite disk was a calendar, or that every round sanctuary had the same rite. It is not confirmed that the draco standard proves a national wolf cult; the Column shows a military standard, and a white wolf beside a warrior is a modern image, not a documented Dacian emblem with a known meaning. It is not confirmed that Trajan’s Column can be used as a map. It is not confirmed that Romanian, or any modern nation, is the biological continuation of one ancient people. It is not confirmed that the kingdom was either untouched by the steppe or created by it.

What remains, after those claims are set aside, is still a large history. A poorly known language, a famous and disputed religious reputation, a kingship that could gather labor for stone and war, a Roman conquest with a dated outcome, a short-lived province of unusual military density, and a genetic record that has just begun: these are enough for a book, provided the book says which sentence comes from which kind of proof. Further excavation in the Orăștie terraces, publication of the Transylvanian Iron Age genomes that have so far been announced rather than fully published, and more cemeteries outside Apulum would each change a specific row of the table. Until they do, the disciplined conclusion is the one the evidence already supports. The Dacians are historically and archaeologically real. Their biological profile is not yet established. Roman Dacia was a mixed frontier society. No single test, text, or fortress can stand in for the others.

Annotated sources

This list names the works on which the factual claims in the book depend. Classical citations refer to standard book and chapter divisions. Where a modern finding is a preprint or an institutional field report, it is marked so that its weight is visible. Conference abstracts and unpublished genome sets are omitted on purpose.

Ancient accounts

Herodotus, Histories 4.93–96, is the source for the Getic reputation, the Salmoxis belief, the messenger ritual, the arrows shot at storms, the Greek story connecting Zalmoxis to Pythagoras, and Herodotus’s own partial skepticism. Strabo, Geography 7.3, especially the chapters on the Getae and Dacians, is the source for the shared language, the regional names, Burebista, Deceneus, the attacks on Celtic groups and Greek cities, the division of the kingdom after Burebista, and the timing of that fall near Caesar’s death. Cassius Dio, Roman History 67, is the source for Domitian’s Dacian war, the defeats of Sabinus and Fuscus, Tapae, and the disputed peace. Books 68.6–14 are the source for Trajan’s two wars, the treaty, the renewed war, the bridge, the capture of the kingdom, and the Roman account of Decebalus’s death. Eutropius, Breviarium 8.6, is the source for the report that Trajan transferred people from across the Roman world into Dacia after a war that had depleted its men. Breviarium 9.15 is the source for Aurelian’s withdrawal of army and provincials and for the Dacia established south of the Danube. Pliny, Natural History 4, and Ptolemy, Geography 3.8, supply geographical names and are not used for population numbers. Ovid’s Tristia and Letters from the Black Sea, and Dio Chrysostom’s essays from exile, are used only as coastal, literary testimony. Jordanes, Getica, is cited as a sixth-century Gothic history that identifies Goths with Getae and enlarges Deceneus; it is not used as an independent Dacian source. Trajan’s Column, dedicated in 113 CE, is the visual source for Roman scenes of the wars, the curved blade, and the draco, and is treated as imperial sculpture.

Archaeology and heritage

The UNESCO inscription of 1999, Dacian Fortresses of the Orăștie Mountains, identifies the fortress group used in the architecture chapter, including Sarmizegetusa Regia, Costești-Cetățuie, Costești-Blidaru, Piatra Roșie, Bănița, and Căpâlna. The National Museum of Transylvanian History reported the 2023 season at Grădiștea de Munte on 4 December 2023, and the June–September 2024 seasons at Sarmizegetusa Regia, Piatra Roșie, and Costești-Cetățuie on 15 January 2025. Those reports are the basis for the ninth-terrace temple, the fire-ended earlier phase, the Piatra Roșie documentation of structures found under Constantin Daicoviciu in 1949, and the newly identified cult foundations at Costești. Edmond Nogyi, Răzvan Mateescu, and Nica Ciubotaru, “Considerations Regarding the Number of Terraces in the Area of Sarmizegetusa Regia,” Journal of Ancient History and Archaeology (2025), are the source for the LiDAR terrace problem and for the warning that terrace counts have changed since antiquity. Virgil Apostol and Răzvan Mateescu, “Grădiștea de Munte – Sarmizegetusa Regia Fortress. Eastern Gate Area – Part I,” Caiete ARA 14 (2023), DOI 10.47950/caieteara.2023.14.03, are the source for the difficulty of reconstructing the pre-Roman fortress plan. Alexandru Hegyi and colleagues, “On the Fringes of the Empire,” Journal of Field Archaeology (2025), DOI 10.1080/00934690.2025.2505818, document new survey at the Roman fort of Ungra and are not used as evidence for a Dacian temple. The Alburnus Maior wax tablets are the documentary source for second-century mining contracts; they are cited from the published epigraphic corpus without inventing tablet numbers.

Genetics

Iñigo Olalde and colleagues, “A genetic history of the Balkans from Roman frontier to Slavic migrations,” Cell 186 (2023), pages 5472–5485, DOI 10.1016/j.cell.2023.10.018, are the source for the 136 Balkan individuals, the limited Italic signal, the Imperial Anatolian-related influx, the post-700 Eastern European-related shift, the 30–60 percent model range, and the present-day Serbian, Croatian, Bulgarian, and Romanian models. That paper is not a Dacian cemetery study. Flavio De Angelis, Ileana Buzic, and colleagues, “On the Edge of Empire: Paleogenomic Insights into Roman Dacia,” bioRxiv, posted 21 April 2026, DOI 10.64898/2026.04.18.719386, are the source for the Apulum–Dealul Furcilor results: 34 sequenced individuals, 14 passing the 10,000-SNP threshold, the sex-biased clustering, the cited haplogroups, and the authors’ own warning that one necropolis may not represent the province. It is a preprint, not a peer-reviewed article. No claim in this book treats it as settled population history for all of Dacia.
